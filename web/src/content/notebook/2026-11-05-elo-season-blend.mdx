## The problem with 42 features

The earlier model had 42 inputs: shooting splits, free throw and rebound rates, home and road splits, scoring variance, and products of one team's offense with the other's defense. Most of them were different ways of saying the same thing, that one team is better than the other, and each one carried its own noise. Gradient boosting will happily split on noise, so a wide feature set was costing accuracy.

The idea was to measure team strength once, well, and then add only the inputs that describe something strength cannot: who is missing, how rested each team is, and the state of a playoff series.

## Elo: one number per team, updated after every game

Elo gives each team a rating that starts at 1500. After every final game, the winner takes points from the loser. How many depends on how surprising the result was and how big the margin was. Three ideas shape the update:

- **Expected result.** Before each game, the rating gap (plus 70 points of home advantage) implies a win probability for each side. An expected win moves the ratings very little. An upset moves them a lot.
- **Margin of victory.** A bigger win moves the ratings further, with diminishing returns: winning by 30 counts for more than winning by 10, but not three times as much. The effect is also scaled down when a heavy favorite wins big, so a strong team cannot run up its rating against weak opponents.
- **Season carry-over.** Between seasons each rating keeps 75% of its distance from 1500, to allow for roster turnover.

## The season-margin blend

Elo is slow on purpose. To give the model a faster read on the current season, each team also gets its average point margin so far. Five games in, though, that average is mostly noise, so early in the season it is blended with last season's average margin. The weight on this season is simply games played divided by 20:

<BlendWeight games={20} />

After 20 games the input is entirely this season. Before that, last season fades out in a straight line. Games played is also passed to the model, so it can learn how far to trust the number.

## The final feature list

Every input the score models see, for both teams unless noted. The same list is used in training and in the daily forecast.

| Group | Inputs |
|---|---|
| Team strength | Elo rating; Elo gap including home advantage (one per game); season scoring margin, blended with last season early on; games played |
| Recent form, last 10 games | Offensive rating; defensive rating; combined pace (one per game) |
| Availability | Points per game of rotation players listed Out or Doubtful on the official injury report |
| Rest | Days since the last game (capped at 5); back-to-back flag |
| Playoff series | Home team's series wins; elimination game; series pressure |

## Results

Two separate tests compared the 21-feature set against the 42-feature set:

| Test | Metric | 42 features | 21 features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walk-forward cross-validation, 3 seeds | Margin error (mean absolute) | 12.05 | 11.65 |
| | Winners picked | 63.8% | 66.1% |
| Held-out 2026-27 games | Margin error (mean absolute) | 12.63 | 11.64 |
| | Winners picked | 59.5% | 61.2% |
| | Brier score | 0.244 | 0.223 |

The smaller set was better on every seed and every metric. A lower Brier score means better-calibrated win probabilities; 0.25 is what a coin flip scores.

## Interpretation

Once team strength is measured well, shooting splits, matchup products and scoring variance add nothing. They mostly re-describe team strength with extra noise. The inputs that survived are ones Elo cannot see: injuries, rest and series state.
